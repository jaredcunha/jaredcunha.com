<Intro>
  Complex web applications hide themselves. Conditional logic, dynamic state,
  and multi-step flows mean that most of the UI is invisible. Debugging surfaces
  bring it back into view, and they benefit developers, reviewers, and
  stakeholders alike.
</Intro>

When we were building IRS Direct File, two bottlenecks quickly became apparent: the user flow’s complexity and the total number of screens. While the UI was quite simple—displaying only one-question-per-page—there was a whole labyrinth of conditional logic that ensured that taxpayers would only see what was relevant to them. Even those pages had various permutations of the content within them. Filing taxes represents the lion’s share of the public’s administrative burden with the Federal Government, a burden that our team was laser-focused on reducing. Seriously, if there was a bullet in a list that didn’t apply to a taxpayer’s situation, we hid it.

That level of complexity, it turns out, is difficult to debug. And that complexity increases with scope. It creates extra, time-consuming work for developers, designers, stakeholders, and anyone else helping maintain accuracy who needed visibility into the application. The deeper anyone goes into the app, the more time it requires from the team. Getting to a particular screen means answering more and more questions in a specific way. We solved this problem in two ways: building an Audit Panel to upload a pre-filled _fact graph_ and building the All Screens page to see everything in a single view.

I didn’t write about Direct File’s Audit Panel, and now I wish I had. But I did write about the All Screens page and how we evolved it from a simple loop to a full-featured tool with filtering controls and layer toggles. I wrote about it in <Link href="/blog/exploring-direct-file-using-the-all-screens-page">_Exploring IRS Direct File using the All Screens page_</Link>. The Direct File All Screens page was essential for everyone who worked on or was involved with the program, and it’s all that is left of it that the public can still see and use.

This year, we modernized the [IRS Tax Withholding Estimator](https://apps.irs.gov/app/tax-withholding-estimator), also known by its unintentionally endearing acronym: TWE. The UI and user flow are very different from Direct File (a constraint we had to work with), and though it’s less robust, it still contains a lot of complexity that handles which fields are shown and how results are calculated. Manually validating content accuracy and debugging can be time-consuming and brittle, so we knew from the beginning that we needed features similar to Direct File. Our stakeholders needed those features too.

Once again, we created these debugging surfaces—specific to our app and specific to what we needed to communicate and to whom.

I wrote an architectural decision record (ADR) about [TWE’s debugging surfaces](https://github.com/IRS-Public/tax-withholding-estimator/blob/main/docs/adr/004-internal-debugging-surfaces.md), some of which I’ll cover here. While our decision to build our debugging surfaces had several drivers, the most important were:

- Developers needed to evaluate that their work was correct
- Stakeholders needed to _see_ how calculations were derived and what conditions made content visible or hidden

Taxes are complicated, accuracy is non-negotiable, and tax code—well, tax code is something else entirely. Unfortunately, there’s nothing we can do about the tax code. But we can help taxpayers navigate it with good content design and usability. Getting anything wrong could create confusion and cause taxpayers to lose trust. At worst, it causes material harm for how people plan and manage their finances throughout the year.

Automated testing alone won’t guarantee accuracy either. Yes, it can help make sure that calculations are right, but it can’t cover all the ways that users are instructed to fill out the form when so much of what they see is based on the information they provide. Even if our testing went that far, our stakeholders and tax subject matter experts (SMEs) still need to see our work.

## The Audit Panel

The Audit Panel is our primary debugging tool and was frequently used by developers, stakeholders, researchers, and designers alike. And what’s really cool, in my opinion, is that it’s available in production! If you visit [TWE](https://apps.irs.gov/app/tax-withholding-estimator), open the console in your browser’s web inspector and enter `enableAuditMode()`. A little gear icon will appear in the top right of the page. Click on that and you can open the panel.

Why would a debugging tool show up in production? First and foremost, it was for external stakeholders and researchers who wouldn’t have been able to access other environments. But also, there’s a benefit for tax nerds and the general public as well. Using the Fact Graph audit panel, it is possible to look up and track facts, observe their value and completeness, and how they are derived. It’s a level of visibility into how taxes work and withholdings are estimated, revealing information that is normally difficult to reach in the dense pages of IRS publications.

Developers can also run TWE locally by cloning [the repo](https://github.com/IRS-Public/tax-withholding-estimator). Setup is pretty straightforward, and documented in the README. The Audit Panel is enabled by default in local development.

Building the audit panel was a fun challenge. Initially, it just sat below the main form. We could have left it that way, but in order to track information across multiple pages, we needed the panel to be visually closer to the form so that users aren’t constantly scrolling up and down. In order to do that, we had to move the panel to the side in a way that users weren’t constantly toggling between information. The panel design created other issues, that we solved in fun ways (to me, at least).

1. Putting the panel side-by-side caused issues with the layout, which we solved by relying on container queries instead of media queries.
2. Persisting the panel across different pages in a natural way is difficult in a vanilla JavaScript, non-SPA application. We managed to address this by storing every state of the Audit Panel in `sessionStorage`. This really works best using a local development environment, which you can do. In production, you’d need to re-enter `enableAuditMode()` on each new page, but all the tracked facts remain stored.
3. Visually aligning some of the panel content with the form can be difficult, so we added the ability to adjust the width of the panel.

The Audit Panel has many useful features. My favorite is how you can explore various facts. But you can turn on a visualizer to see what conditions caused certain fields or inline text to appear. You can export and import fact graphs, which was really useful for debugging calculation issues or manually testing pull requests. Finally, you can also override the date.

## All Screens

The secondary debugging surface is the All Screens page. The All Screens page displays every form field and every bit of content all at once in a single view. This view helps display all the information in the estimator that would otherwise be hidden behind user input or some other interaction.

Like the Audit Panel, this page also includes the feature that displays show conditions. But here, it’s always on.

Unlike the Audit Panel, the All Screens page is only available in the local development environment. This was a deliberate choice because we didn’t want users to accidentally encounter this page and potentially create confusion. In order to see this page, you would need to run TWE locally and visit `http://localhost:3000/app/tax-withholding-estimator/all-screens`.

TWE’s All Screens page is arguably less exciting than the one we built for Direct File, but it is no less useful for how our team was able to operate.

## Debugging surfaces elsewhere

I’m not sure how unique our particular solutions are, but when I showcase them, I often hear from other teams how much they would have loved to have something similar. We were never explicitly asked to build any of these tools. We built them because we needed them, and later learned how much our stakeholders needed them. Do they take time to build? Yes. But once built, they can save time, improve clarity, and increase trust.

Building these pages might be easier to do organically than if you want to seek explicit approval. In every case, for me, at least, the value added has been felt immediately. I think about it in the way a prototype is worth a thousand meetings. As a team, we’re well-attuned to where the inefficiencies and frustrations lurk within our projects. So we ask what layers we can add that would give us the visibility we need. We start small with a proof of concept, and enhance from there.

Perfect isn’t the goal. We’re making features for ourselves and perhaps a small handful of others that solve specific problems that our core user-facing products are not meant to solve. I would even add that using coding agents might help ensure that work on debugging surfaces doesn’t end up siphoning off more of our attention than necessary.

One feature I’ve seen a lot of teams build is what we tend to call _kitchen sink pages_. These pages are exactly what they sound like: a page with ALL THE THINGS. These pages are great because they put everything built in a single page, and they often help uncover spacing issues and conflicts between different elements.

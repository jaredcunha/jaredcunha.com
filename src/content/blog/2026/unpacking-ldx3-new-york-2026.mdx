I have been attending [LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/) events for three years now and I don’t think there’s any other conference I’d rather attend to further develop my skills as an engineering director. The talks gravitate towards the kinds of issues and challenges that engineering leaders are likely to face—and right now, there are many.

This year, we saw a very different format. In 2024, I went to LeadDev and then LeadingEng the following year. There were parts that I liked about both conferences, but I found the single track too limiting and struggled to maintain my energy if a talk didn’t pique my interest or was irrelevant to my day-to-day. The new format solves this by combining the content you’d get in LeadDev, LeadingEng, and StaffPlus into a two-day multi-track conference.

Probably as a surprise to no one, the main thing everyone was talking about was AI. Last year, at LeadingEng, AI came up but it wasn’t really the centerpiece. We never heard much about how different organizations were using and restructuring their teams around it. How quickly things change in a year. And they really did.

I would say that a majority of the talks were AI-positive, which is what I’d probably say about any tech conference. Many of them also acknowledged the issues that AI is also creating for organizations and the individuals within them. What I appreciated most was the aggregate of these perspectives and the stronger framework I have for navigating myself and my team through this sudden and massive shift.

There were so many good talks, and attempting to summarize every single one of them would require more time and attention than I am able to give (the lawn ain’t gonna mow itself!). So instead, I thought I’d pull out my notes from a few talks that gave me food for thought.

## Gergely Orosz: The state of the software engineering industry

Gergely talked about what he learned when he went to some of the biggest AI tech companies:

- Very little code nowadays is getting written by hand at big tech companies and AI startups
- IDEs are no longer that important
- “Code reviews are done for.” “Code reviews are theater.” Honestly, this stresses me out because I’m not in a place where I think it’s wise to forfeit our understanding of how our apps work
- Non-engineers are not shipping production code
- Engineering leadership jobs have gotten much worse: increased pressure, higher expectations, and a reduced need for leaders
- AI fluency and AI positivity will be a baseline for hiring
- Engineers with deep domain knowledge will be in high demand
- Ways to future-proof your career as engineering manager:
  - Build AI infrastructure systems
  - Don’t outsource knowledge
  - Use AI to reduce friction
  - Accept that you’ll do less people management
- New preference for fractional CTOs over full-time

I’m also reminding myself to watch his [Pragmatic Engineer Episode with Charity Majors](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/stop-being-skeptical-about-ai-for) who also spoke at the conference, but I didn’t get to see her.

## Ameya Kanitkar: AI-native development—getting the most out of agents

I found Ameya’s talk interesting though I couldn’t help but think about how going all in would stress me out to no end. While a truly AI-native software development lifecycle (SDLC) could have impressive velocity, I find it difficult to see how teams would be expected to be responsible for so much code and documentation produced in so little time. After all, software is still made for humans by humans with other humans.

- Agents write code twenty times faster, but productivity has only increased 20-30%
- Use high-effort models for the planning phases and setting up the work that matters
- No code before a spec is written and reviewed
- A good target for the length of the spec is 10% of the expected code it would take to build
- No human code reviews. Humans review the spec, not the code
- Check all your specs into version control
- Do not trust single agents. Chain different models
- Test-driven development is not optional when agents are writing the code. Write the tests first
- Agents don’t randomly fail. They fail where context is lost
- Build for agents first. Use AGENTS.md for quality and use monorepos for agent visibility

I caught Ameya’s second session later in the DirectorPlus room, which sparked some really interesting discussion. One thing I found interesting was that most of the folks in the room were straddling agentic coding and spec-driven development, which would be levels two and three out of the five levels he provided for getting a fully AI-driven SDLC. Also, I think not getting to level five is fine.

There was another discussion on hiring junior engineers and many folks who raised their hands were excited about bringing in and growing junior engineers because of the fresh perspective they can bring. That got me thinking that bringing in those newer perspectives would also help more senior engineers remain competitive in the job market.

## Kaya Thomas: Honing the right skills in the age of AI

- Called for needing human-centered engineering
  - Problem-solving focused on developing empathy
  - We need humans to understand human needs
- We need to develop systems thinking
- AI can identify patterns more quickly, but it’s going to be human judgement that matters
- Structure data in ways that make sense because AI will only exacerbate poorly structured data
- Assessing engineering performance
  - What is rewarded in engineering culture? Speed? PRs? Token usage? All tempting.
  - Be mindful of what gets rewarded
  - Help your team connect the dots to the bigger picture.

I believe we’ll be visiting this topic often in the next few years because how we do our work is changing. I can definitely see a shift towards evaluating engineers on their sense of judgement and product sense. Human-centeredness is key to that.

## Nick Means: 114 miles to the final cut

This might have been my favorite talk of the conference. Nick tells the story of how Walter Murch used Apple’s Final Cut Pro to edit the film Cold Mountain back in 2002—a first for a major motion picture. At the time, non-linear editing systems were still relatively new and the wildly expensive Avid suites were the only serious player in town. Final Cut Pro was only a fraction of the price, coming in at about $999.

Murch’s work on Cold Mountain was nominated for an Oscar, though it was The Lord of the Rings that took home the trophy. Regardless, it was an impressive feat and changed an industry. To be an editor meant being part of an exclusive group. Whether they used Avid or still spliced film, those machines were expensive, took up a lot of physical space, and were difficult to learn—all of which were barriers to entry. Now, anyone who could afford an Apple Mac could compete.

It’s important to note that it wasn’t Final Cut Pro that earned Walter Murch the Oscar nomination. It was Walter Murch himself and the skills he developed throughout his career.

When it comes to what set him apart, we can look at the following from most fragile to most durable:

- What you can use
- What you can make
- What you can see

Obviously there’s metaphor there.

## Marcel Weekes: Re-engineering the software development lifecycle

Opening the second day, this was another talk that I really enjoyed, and it contained plenty of nuggets I’d like to take back to my team.

- Treat things differently with regard to building for thinking vs building for delivery. In other words, burn the prototype when you’re done. Don’t try to shoehorn it into a production system
- Code reviews are bottlenecks. Senior engineers are spending a lot of time reviewing code, and they can be reviewing code all day
- Because AI exists doesn’t mean that we need fewer developers. Also junior developers will still have an important role
- Technical interviews need to change. Candidates should be screened for:
  - Engineering judgement
  - Ability to clarify what’s required
  - How they use AI-assisted debugging
  - How they can trust/distrust what agents are doing
  - Product sense
  - Explaining trade-offs
- It might be worthwhile for engineering to pull interview techniques from what the product folks are doing
- Management attention is still finite and that won’t change
- Look for opportunities to experiment
  - Start with a 2-3 person team
  - Set an ambitious timeline
  - Gather regular user feedback
- Owning outcomes never goes out of style. Stay flexible and give your team some grace
- We’re in exciting and anxiety-inducing times

I liked this talk because it felt very practical and close to a lot of ideas I’ve been bouncing around. What I’ve noticed, even now, is that most people in my industry are still figuring things out and believe it’s reckless to let agents rip. So a lot of what Marcel said about experimentation I thought was pretty good. And I totally agree with the need to change how we’re interviewing.

## Sandeep Bharadwaj Mannapur: The accountability gap—engineer governance for autonomous AI

I didn’t end up writing notes about this talk, but he gave an interesting demonstration of his [agent governor](https://github.com/sandeepmb/agent-governor). When using teams of agents, the governor essentially demotes agents when they start making dangerous moves and restricts access or removes it completely.

## Emily Nakashima: The 30-minute MBA for engineering leaders

- Senior engineering leaders only occasionally fail for tech reasons
- Engineering leaders need to build more business skills
- Never invest in any project with a future value lower than present value - don’t forget to think about which interest rate to use
- Hiring slowed down in 2022, and that happened at exactly the same time that zero percent interest rates died, not AI
- Learn the difference between accounting and finance
  - Accounting looks backwards: measurement, verification, categorization
  - Finance looks forwards: forecasting, planning, and investments
- Build relationships with finance, learn to use the language they understand
- AI can make your education more valuable. Our judgement and clarity and decision-making set us apart

I’m sitting with that last bullet point. It’s great and I’ve heard several flavors of that throughout the conference.

## Dan Mahr: Obsessing over AI is optional

This was a great talk for those feeling pressure to become early adopters and burn out or become obsolete. Dan calls this a false dichotomy.

He goes on to clarify that he’s not anti-AI. He uses it often and enjoys everything it can do for his work, but also acknowledged the many, many downsides that AI brings.

- You don’t need to be an early adopter to be effective
- Not everyone can be an early adopter because not everyone has the same space to make mistakes
- Learn from the early adopters’ successes and failures. Let that inform how you might be part of an early majority
- You can upshift or downshift where you want to be with adoption as life demands it
- You can still be an effective manager if you’re not an early adopter. You have to be able to judge if a tool is a good fit for your organization
- Cultivate a culture of experimentation and don’t forget to also emphasize the value of negative results.

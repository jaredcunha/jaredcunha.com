  marketing pitch, but it’s a pledge that few organizations actually put into

Yeah, I did a double-take too. The National Design Studio did indeed publish an article about accessibility, and going against type, it wasn’t raising some canard over how it stifles innovation and ruins aesthetics. It’s titled, "<a href="https://ndstudio.gov/posts/accessibility-matters" rel="nofollow">Accessibility Matters</a>," and it contains some decent points about how to make the web work better for more people. Is it ground-breaking? No. Is it enough? Also no.

The post is written by Ed Coristine (it’s up for debate how much of it AI generated), better known as Big Balls. He is young, about 20 years old. There’s a good chance that, at 20, he is a better programmer than I was when I was his age. In part, that is because I wasn’t a programmer back then. It wasn’t until I came across web standards movement in 2004 that I starting thinking about accessibility, and it probably wouldn’t have been until 10 years later that I was marginally better at. I wouldn’t consider myself a specialist until around 2020. We all come into this discipline differently. But no matter how we do found ourselves here, there is no fast-tracking the skills this journey requires (for more on this, I highly recommend [Beth DeConinick’s "On matters of accessibility"](https://bethdeconinck.com/2026/03/04/on-matters-of-accessibility/)).

That’s why I equate the process of building those skills to learning to play a musical instrument in my post, "<Link href="/blog/building-muscle-memory-for-accessibility">Building muscle memory for accessibility</Link>." No matter who you are, when start to pick up a skill, you’re going to be terrible. Over time, you will be okay and only after many years down the road, you’ll be good. If Mr. Coristine/Balls had recently begun his journey, I’d say his post is a good step. But he’s not a random person. He has a leadership position inside of an organization that has massive influence in how the public will be obtaining services from their government.

To this day, the National Design Studio has launched a handful of websites. Most of them are single-page vanity sites, but they are expanding to agency websites now, such as <a href="https://ndstudio.gov/posts/accessibility-matters" rel="nofollow">CIO.gov</a>. All of them have significant accessibility defects. In fact, when the studio made it’s public launch with <a href="https://americabydesign.gov/" rel="nofollow">America by Design</a>, the internet immediately pilloried its poor quality under the hood, bloated code, and an aversion of semantic HTML so strong that I can only describe as _the `<button>` is made of lava_. And it was the same thing with each and every subsequent launch.

So no, I don’t think the National Design Studio’s post is sufficient because they haven’t yet demonstrated that they are capable of doing the work as part of a launch or any other moment. The Federal Government already had in place one of the most accessible design systems out there: the [U.S. Web Design System](https//designsystem.digital.gov). In addition, it also employed a huge number mission-driven public servants with accessibility skills across various functions. And still, there was room for improvement. After DOGE and mandated Office of Management and Budget cuts, many of those people and the programs they worked on are gone. There’s a lot that needs to be rebuilt from scratch.

There is the pragmatist part of me, though. I do think that having "Accessibility Matters" posted under the design studio’s domain is a good thing. It gives those of us still working in this space _some_ cover to continue the work. Executive Order 14151 caused many companies take their accessibility content offline. Federal agencies eliminated accessibility requirements from contracting language. Time will tell if that moves the needle back in the direction that it used to be, to the time before making websites that anyone could use felt subversive.

But I’m not naive. I don’t believe we’ll see anything like an Accessibility Renaissance, of anything close to one. We know the choices this administration has made, the harm it has done to our communities, our friends, our professions, and so much more. The public knows it too, and recent polling reflects that loud and clear. Administrations come and go, but many efforts that begin in one may continue to the next and into future ones after that.

I worked for the U.S. Digital Service both under President Obama and stayed another three years into the first Trump term. I’m proud of what I was able to do during my time, and some of the work I began endures to this day even though I am long gone. That is all to say, I wouldn’t be shocked if the National Design Studio continued to have a life in the next administration. The Studio’s leadership, staff, or scope may change, but by the time that happens, they will have rolled out a significant portfolio that cannot be cut off and shifted to something created under better circumstances—especially when that’s the primary reason for doing so. And just because I don’t agree (this is putting it mildly) with this administration doesn’t mean that I believe that implementation should be degraded. And yes, I know services have lost funding or have been wholly eliminated—and the damage that causes—but I don’t want that damage to extend to what remains intact, adding yet more work to what already needs to be done.

Until the National Design Studio is able to put words into action, and I hope they do, they are simply words. And there words for _those_ words: performative accessibility, accessibility theater, accessibility washing, etc. Bold, public statements and claims about accessibility are seductive, but most people are ill-equipped to evaluate the honesty to those statements. We will see if National Design Studio takes a corrective course, but if they don’t, they would be only the latest organization amongst a large group of others to accessibility wash their work to an unknowing public.

## The D.E.N.N.I.S. System

In one episode of “It’s Always Sunny in Philadelphia,” Dennis describes his system for seducing women. Amongst his many character flaws, he is a narcissist, so of course he calls it the D.E.N.N.I.S. System.

- Demonstrate Value
- Engage Physically
- Nurturing Dependence
- Neglect Emotionally
- Inspire Hope
- Separate Entirely

I think this same system could be applied to how companies use performative accessibility to lure in customers to purchase products that may exclude a portion of their workforce from achieving their potential.

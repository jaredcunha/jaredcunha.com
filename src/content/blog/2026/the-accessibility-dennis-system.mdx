  marketing pitch, but it’s a pledge that few organizations actually put into
  process—leaving trusting customers out of pocket and users unable to proceed.
  The National Design Studio (NDS) entered this fray recently with lofty claims
  that don’t match its body of work.

Yeah, I did a double-take too. NDS did indeed publish an article about accessibility, and going against type, it wasn’t raising some canard over how it stifles innovation and ruins aesthetics. It’s titled, "<a href="https://ndstudio.gov/posts/accessibility-matters" rel="nofollow noreferrer">Accessibility Matters</a>," and it contains some decent points about how to make the web work better for more people. Is it ground-breaking? No. Is it enough? Also no.

The post is written by Ed Coristine (it’s up for debate how much of it AI generated), better known as Big Balls. He is young, about 20 years old. There’s a good chance that, at 20, he is a better programmer than I was when I was his age. In part, that is because I wasn’t a programmer back then. It wasn’t until I came across the web standards movement in 2004 that I started thinking about accessibility, and it probably wouldn't have been until 10 years later that I was marginally better at it. I wouldn't consider myself a specialist until around 2020. We all come into this discipline differently. But no matter how we found ourselves here, there is no fast-tracking the skills this journey requires (for more on this, I highly recommend [Beth DeConinick’s wonderful post, "On matters of accessibility"](https://bethdeconinck.com/2026/03/04/on-matters-of-accessibility/)).

That’s why I equate the process of building those skills to learning to play a musical instrument in my post, "<Link href="/blog/building-muscle-memory-for-accessibility">Building muscle memory for accessibility</Link>." No matter who you are, when you start to pick up a skill, you’re going to be terrible. Over time, you will be okay and only after many years down the road, you’ll be good. If Mr. Coristine/Balls had recently begun his journey, I’d say his post is a good step. But he’s not a random person. He has a leadership position inside of an organization that has massive influence in how the public will be obtaining services from their government.

To this day, NDS has launched a handful of websites. Most of them are single-page vanity sites, but they are expanding to agency websites now, such as <a href="https://ndstudio.gov/posts/accessibility-matters" rel="nofollow noreferrer">CIO.gov</a>. All of them have significant accessibility defects. In fact, when the studio made its public launch with <a href="https://americabydesign.gov/" rel="nofollow noreferrer">America by Design</a>, the internet immediately pilloried its poor quality under the hood, bloated code, and an aversion to semantic HTML so strong that I can only describe as _the `<button>` is made of lava_. And it was the same thing with each and every subsequent launch.

So no, I don’t think NDS’s post is sufficient because they haven’t yet demonstrated that they are capable of doing the work as part of a launch or any other moment. The Federal Government already had in place one of the most accessible design systems out there: the [U.S. Web Design System](https://designsystem.digital.gov). In addition, it also employed a huge number of mission-driven public servants with accessibility skills across various functions in organizations such as 18F, the U.S. Digital Service (USDS), and the many folks working directly within Executive Branch agencies. Their work was nonpartisan—to ensure that the services that government agencies provide reach the entire public for which they serve, without excluding people with disabilities. Most of those federal workers are no longer around.

DOGE, where Mr. Coristine/Balls was previously embedded, is directly responsible for firing so many of the Federal Government’s most highly skilled technologists, and along with them, its capacity (or want) to support the needs of the disabled. So when NDS claims that it is “redefining accessibility in public technology” when it so clearly hasn’t, I just find it very difficult to take “Accessibility Matters” seriously.

There is the pragmatist part of me, though. I do think that having "Accessibility Matters" posted under the design studio’s domain is a good thing. It gives those of us still working in this space _some_ cover to continue the work. Executive Order 14151 caused many companies to take their accessibility content offline. Federal agencies eliminated the word “accessibility” from contracting language and accessibility statements, referencing Section 508 instead. Time will tell if that moves the needle back in the direction that it used to be, to the time before making websites that anyone could use felt subversive. At least one other small win that happened this week, and I’m not sure if it’s related, is that the VA.gov accessibility statement link in the footer now says “accessibility” again.

But I’m not naive. I don’t believe we’ll see anything like an Accessibility Renaissance, or anything close to one. We know the choices this administration has made, the harm it has done to our communities, our friends, our professions, and so much more. The public knows it too, and recent polling reflects that loud and clear. Administrations come and go, but many efforts that begin in one may continue to the next and into future ones after that.

I worked for the U.S. Digital Service both under President Obama and stayed another three years into the first Trump term. I’m proud of what I was able to do during my time, and some of the work I began endures to this day even though I am long gone. That is all to say, I wouldn’t be shocked if NDS continued to have a life in the next administration. The Studio’s leadership, staff, or scope may change, but by the time that happens, they will have rolled out a significant portfolio that cannot be cut off and shifted to something created under better circumstances—especially when that’s the primary reason for doing so. And just because I don’t agree (this is putting it mildly) with this administration doesn’t mean that I believe that implementation should be degraded. And yes, I know services have lost funding or have been wholly eliminated—and the damage that causes—but I don’t want that damage to extend to what remains intact, adding yet more work to what already needs to be done.

Until the NDS is able to put words into action, and I hope they do, they are simply words. And there are words for _those_ words: performative accessibility, accessibility theater, accessibility washing, etc. Bold, public statements and claims about accessibility are seductive, but most people are ill-equipped to evaluate the honesty of those statements. We will see if NDS takes a corrective course, but if they don’t, they would be only the latest organization amongst a large group of others to accessibility wash their work to an unknowing public.

## The D.E.N.N.I.S. System

In one episode of “It’s Always Sunny in Philadelphia,” Dennis describes his system for seducing women. Amongst his many character flaws, he is a narcissist, so of course he calls it the D.E.N.N.I.S. System, which stands for:

- Demonstrate Value
- Engage Physically
- Nurturing Dependence
- Neglect Emotionally
- Inspire Hope
- Separate Entirely

This system also describes how companies use performative accessibility to lure in customers to purchase their products through hollow manipulation. They’ll advertise how their products are made with accessibility in mind when they are not, knowing full-well that potential customers won’t be able to verify those claims. And then contracts get signed, money gets exchanged, and people with disabilities are left to work within systems they can’t use.

Let’s look at how each component of the D.E.N.N.I.S. System relates to performative accessibility. In other words, the Accessibility D.E.N.N.I.S. System.

### Demonstrate Value

This is the bold public statements, but it goes further than that. A company may start at making claims that their products are accessible. But there are other tactics too. One is publishing an Accessibility Conformance Report (ACR). To a layperson, the mere presence of these documents may indicate an accessible product—that due diligence has been done. Reading and understanding these documents is important because they may point out known issues. But I have also seen instances where ACRs are out of date or inaccurate.

Or take a look at this statement on SaaS product’s accessibility page:

> [Company]’s **audience-facing documents** have high accessibility, with nearly all functionality conforming to WCAG guidelines. There’s room for improvement with flexibility for customers to fully configure accessibility features of their user-generated content.

I bolded “audience-facing documents” because I thought it was curious. This seems like a very lawyer-y way of saying their documents might be accessible, it speaks nothing to the accessibility of their actual product.

Then I have also seen instances where accessibility support is simply just made up. I'll call out MUI’s [Chip component’s accessibility section](https://mui.com/material-ui/react-chip/#accessibility) specifically.

> If the Chip is deletable or clickable then it is a button in tab order. When the Chip is focused (for example when tabbing) releasing (`keyup` event) `Backspace` or `Delete` will call the onDelete handler while releasing `Escape` will blur the Chip.

I’m sorry, but what? The `<button>` can be used with both `Space` and `Enter`. There’s no native handling for `Backspace` or `Delete`, and the component offers zero affordance that those keyboard events have been made available. The delete button on the chip cannot be reached using Tab navigation either.

The Chip is hardly the only component in MUI with accessibility issues. MUI has good brand recognition and it’s widely used on a variety of platforms, so it _feels_ like a safe choice. Yet, many of its components simply do not work with assistive technology, and nor would anything else that uses it.

### Engage Physically

Perhaps with accessibility, this should simply be to engage, since most of our interactions will be done virtually or through digital communication. Engagement could take several forms. Most often, it will take the form of how companies will reach out to potential customers to meet with them and talk about their products. They will affirm how their products are accessible but will be unable to answer any follow-up questions, promise to get back but never follow through.

Overlay companies can be particularly aggressive here because they pitch their products as a silver bullet to all of your accessibility issues—a one-click solution to achieving Section 508 Compliance. Of course, nothing can be further from the truth. Using their products instead of building an accessible one in the first place will not prevent you from getting sued.

### Nurturing Dependence

Also known as lock-in. SaaS platforms may contain a suite of applications with package deal incentives and data portability features that make it difficult to even consider alternatives. Then toss in factors like security requirements, Single Sign-On (SSO) compatibility, feature offerings, cost, and other factors, we are talking about a relatively small pool of options. SaaS products in general have hit or miss accessibility. So if only two or three products meet your other requirements, accessibility is going to be on the chopping block and disabled users are left to figure out their own workarounds.

Even when accessible alternatives exist, migrations can be costly and brittle. That alone might be a reason companies decide to stick with an inaccessible, existing product.

### Neglect Emotionally

The thing about accessibility issues is that fixing them can be expensive. It might require teams to revisit entire features from the start, which also has its own 2nd and 3rd order effects. I’ve seen easy to fix accessibility issues remain in the backlog for years. Years! And it’s not because someone never got around to closing them. Those issues can still be in production. Again, calling MUI out here for having [buttons with focus states that don’t meet color contrast minimums](https://github.com/mui/material-ui/issues/28543).

The Federal Government provides services where there is often no equivalent in the private sector. A government that decides accessibility is too woke has life or death implications for people. The Veteran community has accessibility needs at a rate far higher than the rest of the population, and those disabilities are often related to their service to the country. Additionally, the Federal Government has special accommodations to prioritize hiring Veterans back into the civil service. If we are supposed to honor our Veterans, then we should consider their needs. If the government wants to buy inaccessible software, it’s limiting what jobs people can do. When the government doesn’t enforce its own accessibility regulations, it is taking away people’s autonomy.

### Inspire Hope

And on we go. Another accessibility pledge, another promise. I feel like this is where we are with NDS in the Accessibility D.E.N.N.I.S. System. I hope I’m wrong, as I’ve been wrong before. Maybe we’ll see another post, something to demonstrate forward momentum through a maturity model. Getting to a point where you’re getting things right—or close to right—the first time takes time. My guess is that we’ll get more content before we get results. I’m not calling him Big Ba11s just yet.

Overlay companies take this to another level though. AudioEye claims a 3rd party report found their product detects 89–253% more accessibility issues than their competitors. Their website peppered with all sorts of equally bold claims of multi-hundred percent improvements. Or any of their competitors for that matter. Visit any of their websites, and you’ll see all sorts of pictures and text that look and sound inspirational. There’s only one problem: their products don’t work. And that’s coming from disability communities. Oh, and another one, [accessiBe had to pay a $1M fine over its deceptive claims](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-order-requires-online-marketer-pay-1-million-deceptive-claims-its-ai-product-could-make-websites).

### Separate Entirely

This separation takes two forms. Either a product gets dropped because a better alternative exists. This is the better outcome for the user. One could hope that losing customers would force companies to look themselves in the mirror and fix their accessibility issues. But the sad thing is, that probably won’t happen.

Everything these days is made by huge corporations who have already bought up all of their smaller competitors—companies that have so much market share that there’s little incentive for them to do anything. So that other separation is that disabled people lose their jobs because an ableist environment created for them.

I’m writing this post as a reaction to the NDS article, but mainly because it’s timing just happens to align with a topic I’ve been talking about with my fellow accessibility coworkers for much of the week.

I’m writing this because I get frustrated over why so many people have actively made the decision to neglect learning about accessibility, when it’s part of their job. It’s an active decision to deny people access to their products. But what’s even worse is when companies take the shortcut, learning just enough to feel comfortable marketing themselves when they know—or don’t have the ability to know—how they might be causing real harm to real people.

Accessibility isn’t [Charlie work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8SfPrXYVhWk).

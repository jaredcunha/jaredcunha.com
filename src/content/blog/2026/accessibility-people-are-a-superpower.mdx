<Intro>
  If you have accessibility designers or engineers on your team and you’re only
  limiting them to accessibility work, you are missing a huge opportunity.
</Intro>

If you build digital products, there’s a good chance that you’ve experienced some nagging feeling that something isn’t connecting between the designers and engineers. Maybe it seems the engineers are taking designs as mere suggestions, or maybe it seems like designers are never grounding their work in technical reality, or maybe there’s nobody around to corral everyone together until they’re all aligned. Most likely it’s a combination of the three—and in that case, you’ve got a _mold growth triangle_ on your hands that will lead to tech debt, design debt, and, dare I say, vibes debt.

Consistent misalignment is frustrating for everyone, and it creates the conditions that lead to unnecessary work and rework, mistrust and animosity. Your users will undoubtedly suffer in the crossfire.

But that’s why I believe that centering accessibility can help. Accessibility doesn’t fit snugly into the sole responsibility of design, nor does it fit into the sole responsibility of engineering. More often than not, it wanders that liminal space between the two, and accessibility folks can be the bridge you desperately need. Or maybe tunnel is the better analogy? Tunnels are little more discrete—like accessibility work when it reaches maturity—but either way you have an enduring connection between two previously distant points.

Raise your hand if you ever heard someone cite curb cuts (or SMS or closed captioning) as a feature that was intended for people with disabilities that benefits everyone. Now put your hand down. Actually, I don’t even why I asked you to raise your hand. That only works in a crowded room where I’m trying to make a point. But let’s pretend we’re in a room, there’d be a lot of hands raised. Everyone who has ever talked about accessibility has mentioned the curb cut effect. It’s a rite of passage! Anyway, where I am going with this? Oh, yeah! Like the curb cut, centering accessibility benefits every aspect of your design and development process, whether you’re building an assistive technology feature or not.

Let’s start with recognizing that accessibility is everybody’s job as a truth. One, because it is. Two, because any failure to do so will only lead to ableist solutions, excluded users, and driving the designers and developers who actually care towards greener pastures. Once you’re open to that truth, here is something I’ve observed over the years: teams who have accessibility folks have healthier collaboration than teams that don’t.

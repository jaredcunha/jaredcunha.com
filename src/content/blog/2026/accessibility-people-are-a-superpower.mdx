<Intro>
  People who know accessibility well possess a collaboration superpower. If
  you’re only calling them in to check compliance boxes, you’re missing a
  tremendous opportunity to to improve your entire design and development
  processes, and your team culture.
</Intro>

If you build digital products, there’s a good chance that you’ve experienced some nagging feeling that something isn’t connecting between the designers and engineers. Maybe it seems the engineers are taking designs as mere suggestions, or maybe it seems like designers are never grounding their work in technical reality, or maybe there’s nobody around to corral everyone together until they’re all aligned. Most likely it’s a combination of the three—and in that case, you’ve got a _mold growth triangle_ on your hands that will lead to tech debt, design debt, and, dare I say, vibes debt.

Consistent misalignment is frustrating for everyone, and it creates the conditions that lead to unnecessary work and rework, mistrust and animosity. Your users will undoubtedly suffer in the crossfire.

But that’s why I believe that centering accessibility can help. Accessibility doesn’t fit snugly into the sole responsibility of design, nor does it fit into the sole responsibility of engineering. More often than not, it wanders that liminal space between the two, and accessibility folks can be the bridge you desperately need. Or maybe a tunnel is the better analogy? Tunnels are a little more discreet—like accessibility work when it reaches maturity—but either way you have an enduring connection between two previously distant points.

Raise your hand if you’ve ever heard someone cite curb cuts (or SMS or closed captioning) as a feature that was intended for people with disabilities that benefits everyone. If you were in a crowded room, there’d be a lot of raised hands. But you’re probably not and I’m sorry if I made you do that in public. Everyone who has ever talked about accessibility has mentioned the curb cut effect. It’s a rite of passage! And like the curb cut, centering accessibility benefits your entire design and development process, whether you’re building an assistive technology feature or not.

Let’s accept, as a given, that accessibility is everybody’s job. One, because it is. Two, because any failure to do so will only lead to ableist solutions, excluded users, potential lawsuits, and driving the designers and developers who actually care towards greener pastures. Backfilling those roles is expensive too. If accessibility is everyone’s job, you’ll likely have someone, or preferably a few folks, on the team with the technical skills who is empowered to ensure accessibility is baked into all work from the start.

Accessibility work is often relegated to auditing sites and remediating defects. When that work is complete, focus shifts from accessibility to something else. In actuality, accessibility work is ongoing. Complex problems can come up at any time, and they can cause serious headaches for your team and your users. That’s why you want to have people around who can spot them early, before those problems ever end up in production.

What I’m saying is that you should have accessibility designers and accessibility engineers fully integrated into your team, working on the same things as everyone else—building, not fixing. They are perfectly capable, so let’s stop with this notion that because someone specializes in accessibility, that all their other skills are somehow diminished—or that accessibility skills are only needed at specific phases of project work. I’ll even add that it’s actually part of our jobs as practitioners on the web platform to support ALL the ways users can interact with digital content, but that’s a whole other post.

Something I’ve observed over the years is that teams who have accessibility folks on board have healthier collaboration than teams that don’t. Team members with accessibility skills are naturally a bit more generalist and might have broader awareness of technical and UX considerations. Accessibility designers will be more aware of how semantic HTML and how use/misuse of ARIA impacts users, so they are going to want to work more closely with the engineers to make sure things are done correctly. Accessibility engineers are going to have more awareness of UX issues and limitations that design tools have related to the non-visual experience, so they are going to work more closely with designers to make sure things are done correctly.

That kind of collaboration is a superpower. I’m not saying that teams without accessibility folks don’t collaborate, but the teams that do have them collaborate differently, and more reflexively.

The accessibility engineer is going to want to reach out to designers early on just in case there happens to be an accessibility matter to talk about. The accessibility designer is going to want to reach out to the engineers to see how things are shaping up in the browser, maybe get a glimpse at how things behave using a screen reader. When we’re talking about [shifting accessibility left](https://www.digitala11y.com/connecting-dots-of-an-accessibility-audit/), that is what we’re talking about. With accessibility nicely centered in the design and development process, it’s just part of the work.

During these collaboration sessions, it’s totally possible that specifically accessibility things don’t even come up. And that’s fine because while people are talking, there are plenty of opportunities to solve all sorts of issues without needing to go back and forth or write extra tickets. It’s a chance to go over:

- How the design reacts to different sizes, because you might have five different breakpoints… are designers creating variations for each of those? I bet not!
- Design options right in the browser through co-designing
- Nuances within the design system
- How best to balance an optimized user experience against technical considerations

On top of everything, it’s an opportunity to share knowledge and build rapport. When design and engineering work together, it doesn’t have to feel like a battle.

Another benefit that everyone can appreciate is that this practice leads to fewer bugs and less technical or design debt. That leads to better outcomes for users and happier stakeholders. We often hear that accessibility is too expensive, but I believe that is a point of view that hasn’t considered what real accessibility expertise can bring to the table, nor the business value it can deliver. I’ve seen it so many times: when a person with accessibility skills joins a project, the overall quality of work improves.

Accessibility folks can play a key role in unlocking your team’s potential. If they are only doing audits and remediating defects, you’re leaving your superpower on the bench. Let’s stop pretending that the glasses and suit are an effective disguise. We all see that cape sticking out.

A final note: If you’re an accessibility specialist feeling stuck and any of this resonates with you, bring it to your team. You have a lot more value than this industry would lead you to think—and you will have a greater impact on improving accessibility.

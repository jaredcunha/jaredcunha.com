I know, hahaha, right? That was what I first thought too. But once I took a little more time to consider what that little video clip actually conveyed back to me, I started getting paranoid. If the camera understands that I'm flicking it off, that means it understands every single thing that anyone in my house is doing at all times, and stores it in a way that can be easily queried or analyzed by chatbots.

What's my routine when leaving the house? Am I drinking too much? How often do I clean? Do I seem angry? Have I displayed any subversive artwork? What conversations am I having?

That information can be used to build a profile that screen interactions cannot. And for companies, that is **very** useful information. And for a government that wants to surveil its citizens, you know, to maintain order, that’s also useful. And that would be extra bad if said company and said government were to develop a cozy relationship, say when tax breaks or lucrative contracts can be exchanged for data.

When we had just moved into our house, I was violently mugged a couple doors down by some teenagers playing real-life Grand Theft Auto—wrong place, wrong time. Our neighborhood isn't that dangerous, but it had experienced some break-ins in the past. Judging from the damage to the original front door, it seems like ours was at one point one of those houses (though it could have happened anytime between the 1920's and the day before we moved in). Then we got a dog. So we put in some cameras partly as a deterrent—they placed conspicuously—but the main reason was that I wanted to check in on the dog to see that he was okay when we weren't home or if I accidentally left the stove on.

I was aware of the risks of having cameras in the house that could record to the cloud. We positioned them so they can be trained on entryways, never around the more private areas of the house. The apps weren’t all that sophisticated. At first we could only filter when motion was detected, so if something happened, you mainly had to know around when it happened so you can get to the correct timestamp. Then several years later there were more subtle, but descriptive markers: human, animal, package, but that was it. Now though, our cameras describe in detail everything that happens in the house and organizes into a daily update called Home Brief.

Most of our daily lives are pretty boring, but that’s kind of how life is supposed to be—mostly boring. Even James Bond had to fix the pillows, water plants, and sort out the junk mail. When those moments are narrated back to you in the Home Brief, it sounds depressing. _Hey, the most exciting thing that happened yesterday was someone put on socks._ I guess if I want to impress our new robotic overlords, I should consider conducting a séance or filling the living room with pigeons.

Our Home Brief wasn't even accurate. We do not have a child nor do we have a cat. But now, as far as Google might be concerned, I am a parent and a cat owner. The Verge has a great article about how weird some of the hallucinations got: [I let Gemini watch my family for the weekend—it got weird](https://www.theverge.com/tech/813523/gemini-for-home-google-nest-camera-hands-on).

My particular scenario is all very mundane. [Google’s marketing page](https://home.google.com/get-inspired/bring-home-the-magic-of-gemini/) adds a little more excitement with examples such as a video doorbell message alerting you that someone named Alex is holding flowers, or a video of Sam kicking a soccer ball in the house (be better, Sam!). It’s helpful AND fun! And sometimes those details are useful. I can also see where it might help people with disabilities.

Most of the time, it’s a story of us meandering about our home told by an unreliable and offensively boring observer, delivered daily via push notification. Who the hell actually wants this? Maybe in era of the [unencrypted poop camera](https://techcrunch.com/2025/12/03/end-to-end-encrypted-smart-toilet-camera-is-not-actually-end-to-end-encrypted/), best not to answer that.

The absurdity of the Home Brief feature aside, I still cannot get past all the potential privacy, safety, and First Amendment abuses that putting Gemini or any AI product into our home products can open up. Google removed their "Don’t be evil" motto from the beginning of their code of conduct back in 2018. The other big tech companies never even bothered with that and have been acting accordingly, so please forgive me for having trust issues. Can you turn the feature off? Yes. But do I trust that? Eh…

Google claims that the information it collects will not be used for ad personalization or training data, and that we can opt out or change our storage settings. It's worth noting that [Google hasn't always honored their own privacy policy](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/boards-policy-regulation/google-must-pay-425-million-class-action-over-privacy-jury-rules-2025-09-03/) in the past. So yeah, trust issues.

Everything that Gemini logs is searchable. There's a chatbot where I can ask it more probing questions about the goings-on, the people it sees. I asked it if spotted any minorities around the house and thankfully it says it cannot identify people by race or ethnicity, or that’s the information that the chatbot feeds back to the end user. I asked it a bevvy of other questions, such as if anyone seems to be acting suspiciously or what people in the house are talking about. It says that it only uses video—not audio—and that it doesn’t make subjective observations.

That brings me back to the rude gesture. While it is rude in many cultures, it isn’t universally. Context also matters. Someone can extend their middle finger playfully, ironically, or simply use it point to something. Maybe someone is just using their middle finger instead because of a physical reason. Wouldn’t describing the gesture as rude be somewhat subjective then? I asked Gemini, and it responded with "You’re absolutely correct!"

And that's the thing. This technology is sloppy and inconsistent, and it's managed by a company that has repeatedly violated its own policies. And we're supposed to trust that they won't do anything with this new, very lucrative data source? That they won't stand up to requests from a government casting an ever-widening net on what it considers to be sedition, one willing to use contract cancellations—which Google has many—as a form of retaliation?

I bought the cameras to keep _us_ safe, but I’m reevaluating if they can still serve that purpose, given how competitive the AI space has been this year. It’s _move fast and break things_ at an epic scale, and during a moment where everything feels so dystopic.

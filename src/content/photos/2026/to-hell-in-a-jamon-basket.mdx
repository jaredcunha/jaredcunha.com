<Intro>
  The strange feeling of trying to enjoy yourself while sandwiched between an
  unfolding tragedy back home and one abroad that you narrowly missed.
</Intro>

In January of 2026, we traveled to Spain, spending the majority of our time in the beautiful southern region of Andalucía. The reason for our visit was similar to many of our trips over the last few years: my wife having _a thing_ abroad, so we take advantage of the necessary travel to enjoy ourselves and get away from home.

This trip came with mixed feelings about being away from home at a time when ICE was well into their occupation of Minneapolis (often pronounced “Minnianapolis” by Republicans), as part of Operation Metro Surge. An ICE agent had just murdered Renée Good, and we all saw the video. At the same time, the federal government was actively covering up this horrific crime, telling us not to believe our own eyes. To leave home, to leave the country, in the midst of that evokes a set of feelings I can only describe as complicated.

On one hand, getting away felt like exactly what I should be doing. Had I been home, I would have been spending my days refreshing social media as the latest set of horrors unfolded, unable to escape the algorithm constantly shoving a video of the state executing a woman in front of my face. I know that isn’t doing anything great for my mental health, but it was January and too cold to find outside activities. I would find myself on the couch and eventually reach for my phone. On the other hand, it felt like abandonment. While our democratic freedoms crumbled, I would be eating tapas and drinking vinos far outside the grasp of an authoritarian state that would just as gleefully shoot me, cover up my murder, and smear my name and my family for the crime of protecting my neighbors.

It is now October and I still don’t quite know how to reconcile these opposing thoughts. I think on some level, they are both correct and that is how it is when fascism takes hold. Nothing you do feels right. You either sacrifice yourself or you walk away from others. But perhaps you need to feel both. Maybe it can be like a pendulum, where the energy you put in one direction allows you to put it in the other. Or maybe, that is what I am telling myself to stay sane.

So let’s go to Spain.

When we arrived in Madrid, there were already signs that this trip was not going to be smooth. My suitcase rolled off the carousel with a sizable crack in the hardshell. Mirna’s suitcase was nowhere to be found. We both use AirTags for our luggage, so we knew that her suitcase made it to the airport. Getting someone to find and retrieve it is a different story. We must have spent over an hour on two different lines trying to talk to people who could help us out, coming up short each time. Her luggage eventually did come through though, alone on the carousel, long after the flight info disappeared from the screen. Someone must have forgotten about it, only to send it to baggage claim once they realized what they had done.

Even with that setback, we still had a few hours to kill in Madrid before our train to Granada was to depart. We stowed our luggage in one of those luggage locker rooms near the train station. I don’t know why, but I always find these places sketchy. First, you have to figure out how to unlock the front door. Then, you have to figure out how the lockers work. Believe me when I tell you that whoever designed these things is the type of person who revels in burning ants with a magnifying glass as a full-ass adult.

We took one of Spain’s high-speed trains to Granada, roughly a three-hour ride through small towns and olive groves. The train itself was very comfortable, but the speed was a little unsettling. At some points, we were traveling over 300 km/h, or around 200mph.

That unease became fully justified after we settled in at our hotel and I had a chance to check the news. I just wanted to see if the U.S. still existed real quick, but instead the top story was a [train collision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Adamuz_rail_disaster). In Spain.

The collision happened in Adamuz, right outside of Córdoba. I remembered passing through Córdoba. Frantically, I tried searching for the exact location where the crash occurred and discovered that we were on those very same tracks. Then came the terrifying details. An Italian train heading north derailed. And if a derailment wasn’t horrific enough, a Spanish train heading south collided with the wreckage. The crash killed 46 people and injured many more. Judging from the train schedules, that was likely the next train after ours.

It’s hard not to think about all the what-ifs that might have put us on that train. What if our flight was delayed? We weren’t going to spend any time in Madrid, so what if I insisted on making time to get a nice lunch? I actually did that! What ultimately led to us choosing the train we did, and probably why I am still here to write this, was the simple fact that we wanted to get to our hotel to rest after a long day of travel so that we could make an early morning tour of the Alhambra.

A three-day period of mourning followed the crash. Flags flew at half-mast everywhere around the country, as we noticed from atop the Alhambra. It’s not hard to feel conflicted about doing tourist stuff after such a huge national tragedy. I had to remind myself of what I would do if I were back home. National tragedies are a way of life in the U.S., whether it’s a school shooting, office shooting, or shooting at an outdoor event. We’ve been through fires and floods. I’m not trying to normalize any of these things, but when they happen, we still go and do our life stuff. This was my first time experiencing something like that while in a different country. And it happened right there in Andalucía, right where I had been a short while before.

When you climb up to the Alhambra at 7am after barely any sleep, you will question life. This particular set of events just made those questions real ones. Luckily though, as the day wore on, and as we took in the sights and histories, my brain was able to kick itself back towards normalcy.

The Alhambra is a huge complex that comprises several different buildings. The detail work is unreal, both in its technical achievement and scale. One can spend days here exploring the palaces, fortresses, museums. In addition to the Alhambra itself, the views of Granada and nearby mountains are something to behold.

### Albaicín and Sacromonte

The next day, we decided to sign up for a last-minute walking tour through the neighborhood where we were staying, the Albaicín. The tour also included parts of the Sacromonte neighborhood as well, known for its cave dwellings where people still live today. Both of these neighborhoods are known for their narrow, winding streets, steep hills, and bright white facades. The Albaicín is a bit larger, and it’s the kind of place where you easily wander and get lost. And that is actually by design. Its builders designed the narrow streets and winding roads to break up and slow down invading forces.

As the investigation into the train crash played out, all trains between Madrid and Andalucía were cancelled for a couple of days. These cancellations were creating havoc across the country since rail travel is the primary mode of transportation to get around the country. By that time, there were two or three other train incidents where there were fatalities, leading to even more closures. Now it was affecting flights and rental car availability.

Our next destination was Sevilla, and thankfully trains heading east and west were still running. We needed to get back to Madrid in order to get home, and to me, it sounded like there would be enough time for things to get back up and running. Mirna felt pretty strongly that the rail cancellations would get worse and demanded that I book us a flight to Madrid from Sevilla. Now, I am cheap and I hate dealing with airports, but I gave in. Part of me knew she was right. Reality would later prove it.

## Córdoba

We arrived in Sevilla at night and after a brief sleep, we were on another train for a day trip to Córdoba. This brief excursion almost didn’t happen because the initial train we booked had been cancelled. That defeat was short-lived and I managed to book a different train around the same time.

The weather in Córdoba started off manageable but conditions deteriorated significantly. Our dinky little travel umbrellas were hardly up to the task. I wanted to take more pictures while I was there, but it was a deluge. And it was cold. What saved us was the amazing food and wine at [Casa Pepe de La Judería](https://restaurantecasapepedelajuderia.com/). I still dream about that place, and if I ever find myself back in Andalucía, I will make a special trip to Córdoba just to eat there.

## Sevilla

Sevilla is where we were to spend the majority of our time in Spain. Mirna was attending an interpreter conference in the city, which gave me plenty of time to walk the city and take pictures. Joining me from Lisbon was our friend, Richard. While Mirna was at her conference, Richard and I would walk around, check out some bars, and walk some more. Honestly, I think that is all I would need in life. I would happily put those days on repeat.

That is kind of where it hit me that I love this city. It’s a beautiful place—highly walkable and well-organized. Some streets are so narrow that only the smallest cars could fit through them. Yet, during rush hour, there was little traffic congestion. At least, nothing compared to what I am used to seeing here in DC. I also love Sevilla’s bar culture. I love walking from place to place, grabbing small snacks and then moving on. I love that there are sour orange trees everywhere.

Over the next few days, the transportation woes continued in Spain. Our train back to Madrid was officially cancelled. Thankfully, we had our tickets for a fully booked flight. I’m not sure what we would have done to get back. People attending the conference were all flying into Madrid from all over the world. Some people were renting cars and organizing carpools over WhatsApp with people they’d never met.

Back home, things were getting more dire. Federal agents executed another protester, Daniel Pretti, shooting him ten times. Again, the event was captured on video. And again, the government told us not to believe our own eyes. As I am writing this, those events feel like a distant history. We have been careening ever-closer towards falling under a fascist dictatorship. Fascism is here. That is no longer a fringe theory. There are no other labels you can apply to a system that executes its own citizens, lies about it, and does all it can to consolidate power through fear, intimidation, and violent suppression.

Getting out of the U.S. is a nice reminder of a life that could be. I don’t know what it’s like to live in Spain, but I can imagine that the people there don’t live in fear of their government wanting to kill them, and that a sizeable voter bloc would not bat an eye if they followed through with it. I don’t think that the people there worry about getting shot in a crowd. And I don’t think that people in Spain worry about their country starting wars just because they can.

There was once a time when I didn’t have to worry about some or any of those things. I know others never get to not worry about those things. There is another way to exist, and sometimes travel is a reminder of that.

Though I talk about Spain with such contrast with the U.S., there was a time when the people of Spain did have to fear their government. Spain was under the Franco dictatorship until 1975, five years before I was born. When we make comparisons to these authoritarian regimes, we are trying to send a warning not to repeat the worst parts of human history. We have clear examples of what led to these governments rising to power, and how they consolidated it. Yet, we ignore these warnings because the warnings are scary. That is the purpose of warnings.

On our last day in Sevilla, before heading to Madrid, Richard and I walked around the city one more time. We wanted to cross the Guadalquivir, but there was a half-marathon going on that made crossing the main road difficult. It did, however, provide some opportunities to take some pictures.

## Madrid

As it turned out, we got to spend some time in Madrid after all. At home, there was a massive snowstorm that caused thousands of cancelled flights, including ours. In fact, after getting to the U.S., our domestic flights were cancelled too. At one point, Mirna and I were to be going home to different airports. This snowstorm was unlike anything I have ever seen in my almost forty years of being in the Washington, DC area. There were several inches of snow, covered by three inches of ice. It was known as _[snowcrete](https://51st.news/dc-spent-67-million-cleaning-up-after-januarys-snowstorm/)_ and it lingered for over a month. It was so solid that it broke snow plows. It took me over three days to dig out my car. Trash collection was not possible for three weeks. This was far worse than any blizzard.

The weather in Madrid wasn’t particularly great either. It wasn’t snowing, but it rained to the point that my shoes got completely soaked through. So, I didn’t spend that much energy taking photos.

Madrid brings us full circle. Yes, we are back to where our trip began, but also to what I’ve been thinking about as I am putting these words to the virtual page. Madrid was the power center of the Franco dictatorship. And like so many dictatorships throughout history, this one, too, came to an end. It ended when Francisco Franco died at the age of 83. Fascist systems are rarely carbon copies of each other, but they do have many commonalities. The Franco dictatorship was deeply personalist, as is the regime we have before us here at home. It is gone, but its lessons are not forgotten unless we choose to forget them.

So let’s not forget…

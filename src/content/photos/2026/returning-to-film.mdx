<Intro>
  After years of shooting digital, I returned to film photography. It has forced
  me to take an entirely different approach, and I’m find myself reaching for
  film camera more often.
</Intro>

So, I’m what the kids these days would probably call _an old_. I went to high school in the late 1900’s, and one of my favorite electives was my photography class. I wasn’t particularly creative in the visual arts, but I found myself really drawn to photography. We all rented manual film cameras to learn the essentials of shutter speed, aperture, and ISO. We also developed our film made prints in the darkroom. We only worked with black and white film.

It was never my intention to make art with photography. What I wanted to do was document my band, skateboarding, and hanging out with my friends. I was always the kid with a camera. It was always film, but eventually digital cameras became cheaper, smaller, and easier. It was maybe 2003 or 2004 when I put my Nikon N65 down for good.

I was no longer playing music in my band at that point, and after a few years of carrying various digital point-and-shoot cameras, I purchased my first DSLR. It was a Canon EOS Rebel XSi, and it was that camera that kicked off photography as a real hobby and creative outlet.

One of these, I’m going to have to go back into my archives and put a post together. A photographer’s origin story, if you will. Film, however, takes me back to a time a bit before that.

With my digital photography, I was always chasing the film aesthetic. Though when I review my earlier edits, I was pretty far off. But know that the desire was there. I have a better process now. Still, as much as technology and tooling have advanced, film has a quality that I just can’t replicate using digital. And that quality expands beyond the photo itself. It’s everything from the physical medium through to the process of taking the shot.

For my 44th birthday, I decided to recommit to film. I bought myself a Nikon FE, a camera about as old I was. It has very few bells and whistles. Focusing is completely manual, the maximum shutter speed is 1/1000, and you have to wind the film yourself. But it does have aperture priority, which is an automation I can appreciate, as it becomes one less thing to adjust as I try to take my shot.

I never really paid much attention to the film stock I would buy. If I remember correctly, I probably just bought Kodak Gold, equating _gold_ with _good_. Now, I am paying much more attention to different films. I usually go back and forth between Kodak Gold 200 and Portra 400, with a little Fuji 400 or ULTRAMAX 400. Right now, Portra 400 has been my go-to. Unlike shooting digital, once load a roll of film, you’re committed for the next 24 to 36 shots. I like the versatility of 400 and I have mixed feelings about ULTRAMAX.

There are still plenty of film stocks that I haven’t used yet. I have also been using color film exclusively and I want to dig into black and white some more. One of these days…

One that makes shooting film prohibitive is the cost. When factor in the cost of developing a roll and getting decent digital scans, you’re looking at about $3-ish per shot. It can make you nostalgic for the days when we would cavalierly dump a bunch of rolls off at the one-hour photo and pay maybe five bucks. I definitely miss that.

But I look at the price a little differently. First, when I shoot film, I am taking significantly fewer photos. My shots are going be less perfect because who can afford taking a million photos of the same thing? Not that you’d have any way of knowing on the spot? Those imperfections can be something that we have to live with… or they can add something you might have otherwise overlooked. And fewer shots means fewer photos to cull.

Then there’s editing. I can spend hours editing digital photos and trying to get them to look like film, whereas film already looks like film. All I do is make a couple minor adjustments in Lightroom and I can go about my day. So if I look at the cost of film in terms of saving time, the price becomes much easier to accept. I’ll go for anything that gets my eyes away from a computer screen these days.

Another difference that I’ve come to appreciate is how shooting on my film camera requires me to slow down. Metering takes a bit more time because I have to find the shadows, meter, and then compose my shot. Then there’s the manual focusing. I actually like the way this camera focuses compared to other cameras that I’ve had. I’m currently fixing up my parents’ old Yashica TL Electro X. Focusing on that camera is a matter of making a blurry image clear. On the Nikon FE, you’re are lining up edges so they connect. Once you get the hang up, you can really learn to get focus quickly because of it reduces the information your brain has to process.

I like the way shooting film slows you down, to be more in the moment and pay attention to what’s around you. Digital photography is free after you buy the camera, so you can shoot to your heart’s content. It doesn’t really if every single one of your pictures is garbage. It cost you nothing. With film, you are aware of how it costs money every time you click the shutter. Because of that, you become more methodical.

One thing I love about my Nikon FE is the way it feels in my hands. It’s a tank compared to my Fuji, and to me, that’s a feature. The downside for me is that it doesn’t have the weather sealing that a lot of modern digital cameras have nowadays, including my Fuji. That makes me hesitant to bring it on trips with me. I’ll probably get a film point sometime soon and carry that around.

There’s no instant gratification with film. Depending on my days, I might wait over a month until I can see my photos. Again, this another modern convenience that I can live without. There’s this rush of excitement every time that email comes in saying your scans are ready. I can’t remember half the photos I took, so I like the surprise that comes with seeing them for the first time.

I mentioned how much I like the film aesthetic, but I didn’t say anything about why I like it. For me, it's the softness, the colors, and the grain. But I also enjoy some of the blemishes and imperfections that occur sometimes. A burned edge, a light leak that happens just in the right place… all things never anticipated when you took the shot.

So what’s better? Film or digital? To me, this debate is uninteresting. I like them both for different reasons. Hell, I like iPhone too. As the saying goes, the best camera is the one you have. I’m in different modes depending on which camera I’m using. I might carry both at the same time. Both formats work for me. I enjoy doing photography, and I don’t find myself nailed to one way of doing it.

Recently, I came across a local film community, the [DC Film Collective](https://www.rbrettparsons.com/dc-film-collective), while search for film-related things in DC. I’m glad I did because I’ve learned so much in just the short time since. It’s through this collective that I learned about the [Dupont Photo Walk](https://www.instagram.com/dupontphotowalk/), which isn’t exclusive to film. I went on the November walk and there must have been at least 50 people. I have also been inspired to start printing again. After years of trying to avoid compiling more stuff, I learned to make exception. There’s something special about holding a photograph with your own two hands, and not looking at it on a screen. Three weeks ago, I placed an order for nearly 2,000 of my photos that have been sitting the cloud, now organized nicely in a box that I can share amongst my family and my friends, the way we used to do it.
